\begin{abstract}
Differential Evolution (DE) builds and evaluates its trial vectors independently, which
makes it well suited to GPUs, but a naive mapping of one thread to one individual leaves
most of the device idle. Starting from a CUDA Particle Swarm Optimization code, we
implement DE/rand/1/bin on the GPU with two mappings, one thread per individual
(version~1) and one block per individual with one thread per gene (version~2, following
the design of cudaDEi), together with the self-adaptive jDE. On four shifted benchmark
functions, three dimensions and three population sizes (1440 runs on a Tesla T4), version~2
is the fastest program in every configuration, $1.9\times$ to $58\times$ faster than a
sequential C++ DE (up to $74\times$ for a single function). With Holm's correction over all
tests, the GPU versions of DE/rand/1/bin are less accurate than the sequential DE only with
$N=500$; a sequential DE with the same generational replacement is not distinguishable from
them in any configuration, so the loss comes from the replacement scheme, not from the GPU.
jDE is more accurate than the sequential DE in 17 of 36 configurations. Finally, an
independent implementation reproduces our errors but not those published for cudaDEi.
\end{abstract}

CUDA, GPU, Differential Evolution, Massively Parallel Computing, Global Optimization

\section{Introduction}
Population-based metaheuristics spend most of their time evaluating candidate solutions.
In Differential Evolution (DE)~\cite{storn1997}, each of the $N$ individuals builds a trial
vector from three other individuals and evaluates it; these $N$ tasks are independent, and
inside each of them the $D$ coordinates are processed almost independently as well. This
two-level parallelism matches the hierarchy of a GPU, made of blocks of threads.

Several CUDA implementations of DE exist. cudaDE~\cite{veronese2010} assigns one block of
64 threads to one individual on a fixed grid; FPDE~\cite{dominguez2011} uses a separate kernel
per DE operation; cudaDEi~\cite{qin2012} fuses mutation, crossover, evaluation and replacement
into one kernel, assigns one thread to each gene and chooses the launch configuration from
the problem. Qin \emph{et al.}\ show that the first two designs suffer from many
global-memory transfers between kernels and from low occupancy, and can be slower than a
sequential DE for small problems.

This paper does not propose a new design: our version~2 follows the gene-level design of
cudaDEi. Our contributions are on its evaluation:
\begin{itemize}
  \item two GPU mappings of DE/rand/1/bin and a GPU jDE~\cite{brest2006}, built on an
        interface shared with a sequential reference and checked at every run;
  \item a campaign of 1440 runs analysed with one statistical protocol (Mann--Whitney
        tests with Holm's correction over all comparisons);
  \item a control experiment, a sequential DE with generational replacement, which locates
        the cause of the accuracy differences between the GPU and sequential programs;
  \item a reproduction attempt of the published cudaDEi errors with an independent
        implementation.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:gpu} recalls the CUDA model, Section~\ref{sec:de} describes the sequential
and parallel DE, Section~\ref{sec:exp} reports the experiments and Section~\ref{sec:conc}
concludes.

\section{GPU Programming}\label{sec:gpu}
CUDA programs run on a heterogeneous system: the host (CPU) executes ordinary C++ code and
launches \emph{kernels}, functions marked \texttt{\_\_global\_\_} that the device (GPU)
executes with many threads; functions marked \texttt{\_\_device\_\_} are called from
kernels, and \texttt{\_\_host\_\_ \_\_device\_\_} functions are compiled for both sides.
A kernel is launched on a grid of blocks with the syntax
\texttt{kernel<<<blocks, threads>>>}; each thread finds its position with
\texttt{blockIdx} and \texttt{threadIdx}.

The GPU is made of streaming multiprocessors (SMs); the Tesla T4 used here has 40 SMs,
each holding at most 1024 resident threads and 16 resident blocks. All the threads of a
block run on the same SM, are executed by groups of 32 (warps), can synchronize with
\texttt{\_\_syncthreads()} and share a fast on-chip \emph{shared memory} (48~KB per block).
Threads of different blocks can only communicate through \emph{global memory}, which is large
but has a high latency; its accesses are efficient when the consecutive threads of a warp
read consecutive addresses (coalesced accesses). \emph{Constant memory} is a small cached
memory whose value is broadcast when all the threads of a warp read the same address; reads
of different addresses by one warp are serialized.

The \emph{occupancy} of an SM is the ratio of resident warps to the maximum; it is limited by
the block size, the registers and the shared memory used per block. High occupancy helps to
hide memory latency, but does not guarantee speed. Random numbers are generated on the
device with cuRAND; we use the counter-based Philox generator~\cite{salmon2011}, which gives
each thread an independent subsequence of one seed.

\section{Differential Evolution}\label{sec:de}
\subsection{Overview of the sequential DE}
DE/rand/1/bin evolves a population $\{x_1,\dots,x_N\}\subset[\ell,u]^D$. At each generation,
for every target $x_i$, three distinct indices $r_1,r_2,r_3\neq i$ are drawn and a mutant is
built:
\begin{equation}
v_i = x_{r_1} + F\,(x_{r_2}-x_{r_3}).
\label{eq:mut}
\end{equation}
Binomial crossover mixes $v_i$ and $x_i$ gene by gene:
\begin{equation}
u_{i,j} = \begin{cases} v_{i,j} & \text{if } \mathrm{rand}_j < CR \text{ or } j=j_{\mathrm{rand}},\\
x_{i,j} & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\label{eq:cross}
\end{equation}
where $j_{\mathrm{rand}}$ guarantees that at least one gene comes from the mutant. Genes
leaving the domain are clipped to $[\ell,u]$, and $u_i$ replaces $x_i$ if
$f(u_i)\le f(x_i)$. We use $F=0.5$, $CR=0.3$ and a budget of $10^4D$ evaluations, counted
exactly (the initial population costs $N$ evaluations and the last generation may be
partial).

The sequential reference (C++, one core) replaces $x_i$ as soon as its trial wins, so the
next individuals of the same generation already see the new value (\emph{asynchronous} DE).
On the GPU, all trials of a generation are built in parallel, so the replacement takes place
at the end of the generation (\emph{generational} DE); otherwise a thread could read
$x_{r_1}$ while another one overwrites it. The sequential program also has a generational
mode, used as a control in Section~\ref{sec:exp}.

\paragraph{Version 1: one thread per individual} The direct translation of the sequential
code uses three kernels per generation: mutation and crossover into a trial array, evaluation
of the trials, and selection. Writing the trials to their own array and selecting in a
separate kernel avoids any race condition, and the population stays on the device. This
mapping has two deficiencies. (i)~With $N\le500$ and 128 threads per block, it launches at
most four blocks, so at most 4 of the 40 SMs work (one SM for $N=100$). (ii)~Each thread loops
over the $D$ genes of its individual, so consecutive threads read addresses $D$ apart and the
global-memory accesses are not coalesced.

\paragraph{Version 2: one block per individual, one thread per gene} Version~2 removes both
deficiencies, following cudaDEi~\cite{qin2012}. Each generation runs two kernels:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{kernel P}: one thread per individual draws $r_1,r_2,r_3$ and $j_{\mathrm{rand}}$
        and stores them as one \texttt{int4};
  \item \textbf{kernel MCER} (Mutation, Crossover, Evaluation, Replacement): block $i$ builds
        $u_i$ in shared memory, thread $t$ handling genes $t, t+T, \dots$; each thread computes
        its part of $f(u_i)$, the block sums the parts by a tree reduction in $\log_2 T$ steps
        (Griewank reduces a sum and a product together), and the winner of $u_i$ and $x_i$ is
        written to a second population buffer.
\end{itemize}
The grid now has $N$ blocks of $T$ threads instead of $N$ threads, and consecutive threads read
consecutive genes. The two population buffers are swapped by exchanging pointers (double
buffering). After the last generation, an argmin kernel finds the best individual, so the only
transfer in the timed region is a 16-byte structure. Fig.~\ref{fig:seq} shows one run.

Two differences with cudaDEi remain. Kernels P and MCER run one after the other in one stream,
whereas cudaDEi overlaps them on two streams. And the shift vector stays in constant memory as
in version~1, which no longer suits version~2: thread $j$ reads $o_j$, so a warp reads 32
different addresses and these reads are serialized; reading $o$ through the read-only cache
would avoid it. We did not measure the effect of either choice.

\paragraph{Block size} $T$ is a power of two chosen automatically: $T=32$ if $D\le32$ or
$N\ge4\times\#\mathrm{SM}$, and $T=64$ otherwise. The rule comes from a measured sweep
(Section~\ref{sec:time}).

\paragraph{Variants} The same organization supports current-to-best/1,
$v_i = x_i + F(x_{\mathrm{best}}-x_i) + F(x_{r_1}-x_{r_2})$, and jDE~\cite{brest2006}, where
every individual carries its own $F_i$ and $CR_i$ (initially $0.5$ and $0.9$); with
probability $0.1$ each, kernel P proposes $F'\sim\mathcal{U}(0.1,1]$ and
$CR'\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1]$, and the new values survive only if the trial wins.

\paragraph{Built-in checks} Outside the timed region, every run checks that all genes are
finite and inside the domain and that the evaluation count does not exceed the budget.
Version~2 and the variants also check that the GPU argmin equals a CPU minimum and that the
fitness obtained by the parallel reduction equals a CPU evaluation of the same vector, within
$10^{-9}\max(1,|f|)$. All the recorded runs passed these checks.

\section{Experimental Results}\label{sec:exp}
\subsection{Setup}
GPU: NVIDIA Tesla T4 (Google Colab, 40 SMs), \texttt{nvcc -O3 -arch=sm\_75}; CPU: Intel
Xeon at 2.00~GHz, one core, \texttt{g++ -O2}, without explicit vectorization or
multithreading. All computations are in double precision, which the T4 executes at 1/32 of
its single-precision rate. The full campaign (CUDA~13) runs the sequential DE, versions 1 and
2 and jDE on 4 functions, $D\in\{10,50,100\}$ and $N\in\{50,100,500\}$, with seeds 1 to 10:
1440 runs. The block-size sweep (Table~\ref{tab:bloc}) was measured earlier with CUDA~12.

\emph{Timing.} The sequential time runs from the initialization of the population to the end
of the main loop. The GPU time, measured with CUDA events, runs from the initialization of the
random generators and of the population to the copy of the best individual; it excludes the
creation of the CUDA context, the allocations, the copy of $o$ and the final copy of the
population used by the checks. Identical GPU runs differ by less than 1\,\% in time, whereas
the sequential times vary by 10 to 17\,\% between Colab sessions; speedups are therefore given
with two significant digits.

\emph{Statistics.} Each GPU algorithm is compared with the sequential DE by a two-sided
Mann--Whitney test~\cite{mann1947} on the 10 final errors, with Holm's
correction~\cite{holm1979} over the 108 tests of the campaign (3 algorithms $\times$ 36
configurations), $\alpha=0.05$. Runs share a seed but not a random stream, so they are not
paired and the signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945} does not apply. Kruskal--Wallis
tests~\cite{kruskal1952} are corrected over the 36 configurations. Two identical samples give
$p=1$. A non-significant result with 10 runs means that no difference was detected, not that
the algorithms are equivalent.

\subsection{Benchmark functions}
With $z=x-o$ (and $z=x-o+1$ for Rosenbrock), following CEC~2005~\cite{suganthan2005}:
\begin{align*}
f_{\mathrm{Sph}}(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{D} z_j^2,\\
f_{\mathrm{Ros}}(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{D-1} 100\,(z_j^2-z_{j+1})^2+(z_j-1)^2,\\
f_{\mathrm{Gri}}(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{j} \frac{z_j^2}{4000} - \prod_{j}\cos\frac{z_j}{\sqrt{j}} + 1,\\
f_{\mathrm{Ras}}(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{j} z_j^2 - 10\cos(2\pi z_j) + 10,
\end{align*}
on $[-100,100]^D$ (Sphere, Rosenbrock), $[-600,600]^D$ (Griewank) and $[-5,5]^D$ (Rastrigin).
The shift $o$ is drawn uniformly in 80\,\% of the domain and the CEC bias is not added. Each
program returns the error $f(x)-f(o)$. Its resolution differs between functions: for Griewank
($1-\prod\cos$) and Rastrigin ($10-10\cos$) the cancellation makes any error below about
$10^{-16}$ equal to 0, whereas for Sphere an error of 0 means $x=o$ exactly in double precision.
The functions are shifted but not rotated; Sphere and Rastrigin are separable and Griewank
becomes nearly separable as $D$ grows.

\subsection{Running time}\label{sec:time}
\paragraph{Campaign} Table~\ref{tab:camp} and Fig.~\ref{fig:camp}. Version~2 is the fastest
program in all 36 configurations. Its speedup over the sequential DE grows with $D$ and $N$,
from $1.9\times$ ($D=10$, $N=50$) to $58\times$ ($D=100$, $N=500$) on times pooled over the four
functions, and from $1.5\times$ to $74\times$ for single functions. Version~1 is slower than the
sequential DE with $N=50$ and $D=10$ ($0.81\times$), stays below $2.3\times$ with $N\le100$, and
reaches $6\times$ to $9.5\times$ with $N=500$, when it launches four blocks. Version~2 is thus up
to about $15\times$ faster than version~1 ($D=100$, $N=100$). Since version~1 was not tuned
(blocks of 32 threads would use four SMs instead of one for $N=100$), this ratio measures the
benefit of the gene-level mapping over this baseline, not over the best one-thread-per-individual
code. jDE costs 10 to 33\,\% more time than version~2, depending on the configuration.

These speedups are obtained at equal budget, not at equal quality: where the GPU versions are
less accurate (Section~\ref{sec:quality}), the sequential DE gets more from the same budget.
With $D=10$ the sequential DE runs in 0.03~s, less than the creation of a CUDA context, so the
small speedups of this dimension only matter when the GPU is already initialized.

\paragraph{Block size} Table~\ref{tab:bloc} gives the time of version~2 for several block
sizes. For $D=50$ and $100$, blocks of 64 to 512 threads all reach 100\,\% theoretical
occupancy, yet 128 and 256 threads are always slower than the best of 32 and 64: with $D=50$
and $T=256$, 80\,\% of the threads have no gene, and every extra thread lengthens the reduction
and the synchronizations. The differences between 32 and 64 threads (6 to 25\,\%) are well
above the run-to-run variation of the GPU. Blocks of 32 threads win with $N=500$ (12.5 blocks

lie between $N=100$ and $500$, and the branch $D\le32$ was not swept.

\subsection{Solution quality}\label{sec:quality}
\paragraph{GPU versions against the sequential DE} Tables~\ref{tab:err50} to \ref{tab:err500}
give the final errors. Against the sequential DE, version~1 is better in 0, not different in
31 and worse in 5 configurations; version~2 in 0, 32 and 4. All these differences occur with
$N=500$: Sphere for the three dimensions and Griewank for $D=50$ and $100$ (version~2 is not
significantly worse on Griewank with $D=100$ after correction). Kruskal--Wallis separates the
three implementations of DE/rand/1/bin in exactly these 5 configurations. With $N=500$ the
budget allows only 200 to 2000 generations and DE/rand/1/bin has not converged
($0.07$ on Sphere with $D=100$); the sequential error is then 1.3 to 4 times lower. With
$N\le100$ no difference is detected.

\paragraph{Generational control} The sequential DE replaces an individual as soon as its trial
wins, which propagates improvements within a generation; the GPU versions cannot. To test whether
this explains the differences, we ran the sequential program in generational mode (trials read
the previous population, winners go to a second one) on the 36 configurations with the same
seeds, 360 runs. Table~\ref{tab:gen} summarizes the tests. After correction, the generational
sequential DE differs from the asynchronous one in 6 configurations, the 5 above and Sphere with
$D=100$, $N=100$ (an error of $10^{-28}$ against 0, without practical meaning), and it is not
distinguishable from version~1 or version~2 in any configuration. The accuracy losses of the
GPU versions are therefore explained by the generational replacement, not by the GPU
implementation. Its times were measured on another machine and are not comparable with the
campaign.

\paragraph{jDE} jDE is better than the sequential DE in 17 configurations, not different in 18
and worse in one (Sphere, $D=100$, $N=50$: $7\times10^{-28}$ against 0). It also fails once
without significance: on Griewank with $D=100$, $N=50$, some runs do not reach the optimum
(mean 0.044 against 0). With $N\le100$ its gain comes mostly from Rastrigin, which it solves
for $D=50$ and $100$ where DE/rand/1/bin stays near 165 and 535: on a separable function a
small $CR$ is favourable (Table~\ref{tab:var}), and jDE adapts $CR$ towards such values. With
$N=500$ it is better in all 12 configurations, including the non-separable Rosenbrock
($104$ against $2.2\times10^4$ for $D=100$): there the budget allows few generations, and the
adapted $F_i$ and $CR_i$ converge much faster than the fixed $F=0.5$, $CR=0.3$. Counting as a
success a final error below $10^{-8}$, as Qin \emph{et al.}, the sequential DE and both GPU
versions succeed in 140 of their 360 runs and jDE in 223. These gains are algorithmic: they
are not a property of the GPU, and a sequential jDE should show them as well.

\paragraph{DE variants} Table~\ref{tab:var} compares six variants of the GPU program for
$D=50$, $N=100$ (10 runs): rand/1 with $CR\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.9\}$, current-to-best/1 (ctb1)
and jDE. With $CR=0.3$ the variant program reproduces version~2 bit for bit. Kruskal--Wallis
detects a difference on Sphere, Rosenbrock and Rastrigin ($p<10^{-6}$); all variants solve
Griewank. On Rastrigin a small $CR$ is best ($CR=0.1$ reaches $10^{-11}$), on the
non-separable Rosenbrock a large one ($CR=0.9$). ctb1 converges prematurely (mean 1726,
median 341 on Rosenbrock) and costs about 40\,\% more time.

\subsection{Comparison with cudaDEi}
Qin \emph{et al.}~\cite{qin2012} use the same algorithm, parameters, functions and budget, with
$N$ up to 1000, 25 runs and a GTX~560. Their times are not comparable with ours: the GPUs are
seven years apart, their sequential DE runs on another CPU, and the precision and timed region
are not reported. Their errors are, and Table~\ref{tab:qin} shows two gaps. On
Sphere with $N=500$ they report a success rate of 1 for the three dimensions, whereas our DE has
not converged. On Rastrigin with $D=50$, $N=100$ they report 2.94 against 165. An independent
NumPy implementation of DE/rand/1/bin, written from the equations of Section~\ref{sec:de},
reproduces our values in both cases (for Rastrigin, whatever the bound handling), and still does not reach the
published Sphere errors if the domain is reduced to $[-5.12,5.12]^D$. The published results are
therefore not reproducible from the paper's description; we could not identify the difference.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conc}
Mapping one individual to one thread gives a correct GPU DE but not a fast one: with up to 500
individuals, most of the 40 SMs of a T4 stay idle. Mapping one individual to one block and one
gene to one thread, fusing the four DE operators and keeping the population and the search for
the best individual on the device makes the GPU program faster than the sequential DE in every
configuration, by $1.9\times$ to $58\times$ on pooled times. The GPU versions lose accuracy only
with $N=500$, and a generational sequential DE loses the same accuracy, which locates the cause
in the replacement scheme. The self-adaptive jDE runs at almost the same speed and is the most
accurate program. Measured timings, not theoretical occupancy, determined the block size.

\emph{Limitations.} The speedups come from one GPU, in double precision, against a single-core
sequential code compiled with \texttt{-O2}, and are at equal budget, not at equal quality.
Version~1 was not tuned. No profiler was used, so the explanations based on occupancy and memory
accesses are consistent with the timings but not measured. Quality was compared on 10 runs per
configuration, on shifted but unrotated functions. The block-size rule was fitted on 12 cases.
The cudaDEi errors could not be reproduced.

\emph{Future work.} Overlapping kernels P and MCER on two streams; reading $o$ through the
read-only cache; warp-level reductions (\texttt{\_\_shfl\_down\_sync}) and several individuals per
block when $D$ is small; profiling with Nsight Compute; a sequential jDE and effect sizes;
rotated and composite functions and modern adaptive DE (SHADE, L-SHADE).

The code, the raw results of the 1440 runs and of the control, and the analysis scripts are
available at \url{https://github.com/amenacer/DE_CUDA}.
