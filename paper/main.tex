\begin{abstract}
Differential Evolution (DE) is a population-based optimizer whose individuals build
and evaluate their trial vectors independently, which makes it a natural candidate for
graphics processors. Starting from a CUDA implementation of Particle Swarm Optimization
provided in the course, we design two GPU versions of DE/rand/1/bin ($F=0.5$, $CR=0.3$)
and a self-adaptive variant (jDE), and compare them with a sequential C++ DE on four
shifted benchmark functions (Sphere, Rosenbrock, Griewank, Rastrigin), with
$D\in\{10,50,100\}$, $N\in\{50,100,500\}$, a budget of $10^4D$ evaluations and 10 runs per
configuration: 1440 runs on an NVIDIA Tesla T4. Version~1 maps one thread to one
individual; it occupies only a few of the 40 streaming multiprocessors and runs from
$0.7\times$ to $9.5\times$ the speed of the sequential code. Version~2 maps one block to one
individual and one thread to one gene, fuses mutation, crossover, evaluation and
replacement into a single kernel with a shared-memory tree reduction, and keeps the
population and the best-individual search on the GPU; it is the fastest program in all
36 configurations, from $1.5\times$ to $74.5\times$ faster than the sequential DE. Against the
sequential DE (Mann--Whitney test, Holm correction), versions 1 and 2 show no significant
difference in quality in 29 and 27 configurations and are less accurate in 7 and 8, mostly
with $N=500$; a generational sequential DE run as a control reproduces these differences,
which therefore come from the replacement scheme, not from the GPU.
jDE, 10 to 33\,\% slower than version~2, is significantly more accurate than the sequential
DE in 19 configurations and solves Rastrigin for $D=50$ and $D=100$ with $N=100$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Population-based metaheuristics spend most of their time evaluating candidate
solutions. In Differential Evolution (DE) \cite{storn1997}, each of the $N$ individuals
produces one trial vector per generation from three other individuals of the
current population; these $N$ constructions and evaluations are independent, and
inside each of them the $D$ coordinates are processed almost independently as well.
This two-level parallelism (individuals, then genes) fits the hierarchy of a GPU
(blocks, then threads).

This work was carried out in the massively parallel computing course of the Master~2
IMDS. The starting point is a CUDA implementation of Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)
\cite{kennedy1995} provided by the course, which we transform into a DE, following the
approach of Qin et~al.~\cite{qin2012}. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a diagnosis of the PSO code (race condition on global temporaries, wrong
        Griewank product, missing shift and bounds, best particle computed on the
        CPU at every iteration), which motivated a common, tested interface shared by
        the sequential and GPU programs;
  \item a GPU implementation of DE/rand/1/bin that maps one block to one individual and
        one thread to one gene (version~2), compared with the direct one-thread-per-individual
        mapping (version~1) and validated against a sequential C++ implementation with
        statistical tests and a generational control;
  \item a measured analysis of the block size, occupancy and speedup on a Tesla T4;
  \item a comparison of DE variants (crossover rate, current-to-best/1, jDE \cite{brest2006})
        running on the GPU.
\end{itemize}
Section~\ref{sec:gpu} recalls the CUDA programming model, Section~\ref{sec:de} describes
the sequential and parallel DE, Section~\ref{sec:exp} reports the experiments, and
Section~\ref{sec:conc} concludes.

\section{GPU Programming}\label{sec:gpu}
A CUDA GPU is made of streaming multiprocessors (SMs); the Tesla T4 used here has
40 SMs, each running at most 1024 resident threads and offering 48~KB of shared
memory per block. A kernel is launched on a grid of blocks; the threads of a block
run on the same SM, are scheduled by groups of 32 (warps), can synchronize with
\texttt{\_\_syncthreads()} and share a fast on-chip memory. Global memory is large
but slow; it is used efficiently when consecutive threads read consecutive
addresses (coalesced accesses). Constant memory is cached and broadcast to all the
threads of a warp that read the same address. Random numbers are generated on the
device with cuRAND; we use the counter-based Philox generator \cite{salmon2011}, which
gives each thread an independent subsequence of a single seed.

\section{Differential Evolution}\label{sec:de}
\subsection{Overview of the sequential DE}
DE/rand/1/bin keeps a population $\{x_1,\dots,x_N\}\subset[\ell,u]^D$. At each
generation, for every individual $i$, three distinct indices $r_1,r_2,r_3\neq i$ are
drawn and a mutant is built:
\begin{equation}
v_i = x_{r_1} + F\,(x_{r_2}-x_{r_3}).
\label{eq:mut}
\end{equation}
Binomial crossover mixes $v_i$ and $x_i$ gene by gene:
\begin{equation}
u_{i,j} = \begin{cases} v_{i,j} & \text{if } \mathrm{rand}_j < CR \text{ or } j=j_{\mathrm{rand}},\\
x_{i,j} & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\label{eq:cross}
\end{equation}
where $j_{\mathrm{rand}}$ guarantees that at least one gene comes from the mutant.
Genes leaving the domain are clipped to $[\ell,u]$. Selection keeps the better of the
two: $x_i \leftarrow u_i$ if $f(u_i)\le f(x_i)$. Unless stated otherwise, we use $F=0.5$ and $CR=0.3$,
and a budget of $10^4 D$ evaluations of $f$, counted exactly (the initial
population costs $N$ evaluations and the last generation may be partial).

The sequential reference (C++, one core) replaces $x_i$ as soon as its trial wins, so
later individuals of the same generation already see the new value. On the GPU the
replacement must take place at the end of the generation (a \emph{generational}
DE), otherwise a thread could read $x_{r_1}$ while another thread overwrites it. The
two programs are therefore two variants of the same algorithm; we compare them
statistically in Section~\ref{sec:exp}.

\subsection{Massively parallel DE}
\paragraph{Use cases} Fig.~\ref{fig:usecase} gives the UML use case diagram of the
software. The experimenter, or the campaign script, runs the sequential DE, a GPU
version or a variant; each run takes a function, $D$, $N$ and a seed, checks its own
health and appends one row to a CSV file shared by all programs, which the analysis
scripts read to produce the tables, figures and tests of Section~\ref{sec:exp}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{uml_use_case.pdf}
\caption{UML use case diagram of the DE\_CUDA software.}
\label{fig:usecase}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Common interface} The four functions, their domains, the shift vector $o$
and the CSV format are written once in a header used by the sequential and the GPU
programs (\texttt{\_\_host\_\_ \_\_device\_\_} functions). The shift is generated by a
deterministic generator (splitmix64) seeded with the function and the dimension, so
both programs optimize exactly the same problem. Each function returns the error
$f(x)-f(x^\ast)$, which is $0$ at the optimum.

\paragraph{Version 1 (one thread per individual)} Three kernels per generation:
mutation and crossover into a separate trial array, evaluation of the trials, and
selection. Because trials are written in their own array and selection runs in a
separate kernel, there is no race condition. The shift vector lives in constant
memory and the population never leaves the GPU; only the $N$ final fitness values
are copied back.

\paragraph{Version 2 (one block per individual, one thread per gene)} Two kernels
per generation:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{kernel P}: one thread per individual draws $r_1,r_2,r_3$ and
        $j_{\mathrm{rand}}$ and stores them as an \texttt{int4};
  \item \textbf{kernel MCER} (Mutation, Crossover, Evaluation, Replacement): block $i$
        builds trial $u_i$ in shared memory, thread $t$ handling genes
        $t, t+T, t+2T,\dots$; each thread computes its part of $f(u_i)$ and the block
        sums them by a tree reduction in $\log_2 T$ steps (Griewank needs a sum and
        a product, reduced together); thread 0 compares with $f(x_i)$ and the block
        writes the winner into a second population buffer.
\end{itemize}
The two population buffers are swapped by exchanging pointers (double buffering),
which removes both the copy and the race condition. The best individual is found by
an argmin reduction kernel (512 threads, ties broken by the smallest index), so the
only transfer inside the timed region is a 16-byte structure. Consecutive threads read
consecutive genes, so global-memory accesses are coalesced, whereas in version~1
threads read with a stride of $D$. The shift vector stays in constant memory, as in
version~1, but this choice no longer suits version~2: thread $j$ reads $o_j$, so the threads
of a warp read different addresses and the constant cache serializes these reads, whereas
in version~1 all the threads read the same $o_j$ at the same time and the value is
broadcast. Reading $o$ from global memory through the read-only cache would avoid the
serialization; we did not measure the difference. Unlike cudaDEi~\cite{qin2012}, kernels P
and MCER run one after the other in the default stream; overlapping them on two streams is
left for future work. Fig.~\ref{fig:seq} gives the UML sequence diagram of
one run of version~2, from the command line to the CSV row.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{uml_sequence_v2.pdf}
\caption{UML sequence diagram of one run of version~2. Inside the main loop, the host
only launches kernels and swaps two pointers; the population never leaves the GPU, and
the only transfer inside the timed region is the 16-byte best individual.}
\label{fig:seq}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Block size} The number of threads per block $T$ (a power of two) is chosen
automatically: $T=32$ if $D\le 32$ or $N\ge 4\times\#\mathrm{SM}$, and $T=64$
otherwise. The rule comes from a measured sweep (Section~\ref{sec:exp}).

\paragraph{Variants} The same organization supports current-to-best/1,
$v_i = x_i + F(x_{\mathrm{best}}-x_i) + F(x_{r_1}-x_{r_2})$, where $x_{\mathrm{best}}$
is found by the argmin kernel before kernel MCER, and jDE \cite{brest2006}, where every
individual carries its own $F_i$ and $CR_i$ (initially $0.5$ and $0.9$); with
probability $0.1$ each, kernel P proposes $F'\sim\mathcal{U}(0.1,1]$ and
$CR'\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1]$, and the new values survive only if the trial wins.

\paragraph{Built-in checks} Every run checks, outside the timed region, that all genes
are finite and inside the domain and that the evaluation count does not exceed the
budget. Version~2 and the variants also check that the GPU argmin equals a CPU
minimum and that the fitness obtained by the parallel reduction equals the CPU
evaluation of the same vector (difference below $10^{-9}\max(1,|f|)$, i.e.\ relative
far from the optimum and absolute near it). Every version~2
and variant run we recorded passed these checks.

\section{Experimental Results}\label{sec:exp}
\subsection{Setup}
GPU: NVIDIA Tesla T4 (Google Colab, 40 SMs); CPU: Intel Xeon @ 2.00~GHz (one core
for the sequential program); \texttt{nvcc -O3 -arch=sm\_75} with CUDA 12 for the
validation experiments (Tables I to IV) and CUDA 13 for the full campaign, whose
sequential code is compiled with \texttt{g++ -O2} (one core, no explicit vectorization or
multithreading): all speedups are relative to this baseline. All computations are in
double precision, which the T4 executes at 1/32 of its single-precision rate; this
penalizes the GPU. GPU times are measured with CUDA events from the initialization of
the random generators and of the population to the copy of the best individual; the
creation of the CUDA context, the allocations, the copy of the shift vector and the final
copy of the population used for the checks are excluded. Budget: $10^4D$ evaluations. Quality is
compared on 10 independent runs (seeds 1 to 10), timings are means of 3 or 5 runs;
each table gives its own $N$ and $D$. Results are given as mean $\pm$ standard
deviation of the final error (population standard deviation in the validation tables of
versions 1 and 2, sample standard deviation in the full campaign). The full campaign covers $N\in\{50,100,500\}$ and $D\in\{10,50,100\}$. Two samples
are compared with the Mann--Whitney test \cite{mann1947}, several with
Kruskal--Wallis \cite{kruskal1952}. When several algorithms are compared with the same
reference in one configuration, Holm's correction \cite{holm1979} is applied within that
configuration; Kruskal--Wallis counts over the 36 configurations use raw $p$-values. A non-significant test with 10 runs means that no difference was
detected, not that the two samples are equivalent. Runs of two algorithms share a
seed but not a random stream, so they are not paired and the Wilcoxon signed-rank
test \cite{wilcoxon1945} does not apply; the Mann--Whitney test is the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

\subsection{Benchmark functions}
With $z=x-o$ (and $z=x-o+1$ for Rosenbrock), following CEC~2005 \cite{suganthan2005}:
\begin{align*}
f_{\mathrm{Sph}}(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{D} z_j^2,\\
f_{\mathrm{Ros}}(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{D-1} 100\,(z_j^2-z_{j+1})^2+(z_j-1)^2,\\
f_{\mathrm{Gri}}(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{j} \frac{z_j^2}{4000} - \prod_{j}\cos\frac{z_j}{\sqrt{j}} + 1,\\
f_{\mathrm{Ras}}(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{j} z_j^2 - 10\cos(2\pi z_j) + 10,
\end{align*}
on $[-100,100]^D$ (Sphere, Rosenbrock), $[-600,600]^D$ (Griewank) and $[-5,5]^D$ (Rastrigin).
The shift $o$ is drawn uniformly in 80\,\% of the domain; the CEC bias is not added,
so that small errors are not absorbed by it. The resolution remains limited by double
precision: for Griewank, $1-\prod\cos$ cancels below about $10^{-16}$, so an error of 0 in
the tables means an error below this resolution, not $x=o$ exactly. The functions are
shifted but not rotated; Sphere and Rastrigin are separable, and Griewank becomes nearly
separable as $D$ grows.

\subsection{Results}
\paragraph{Correctness of version 1} The same seed gives the same result. Five
identical runs differ by at most 0.14\,\% in time. Against the sequential program
($N=50$, 10 runs), no significant difference was detected: both solve Sphere and
Rastrigin exactly for $D=10$; Rosenbrock $D=10$ gives $2.03\pm1.62$ (sequential) and
$0.72\pm1.05$ (GPU), $p=0.089$; Rastrigin $D=50$ gives $151.4\pm6.6$ and $145.6\pm6.6$,
$p=0.140$. The Rosenbrock case (a factor 3 in mean) is close to the threshold: with 10 runs the test has little power, so this validation shows no detected difference, not equivalence.

\paragraph{Speedup of version 1} Table~\ref{tab:sp1} and Fig.~\ref{fig:sp1}. With
$N=100$ and 128 threads per block, version~1 launches a single block, i.e.\ it uses one
SM out of 40, and each thread loops over $D$ genes with strided accesses; the speedup
stays between $0.98\times$ and $2.3\times$.

\paragraph{Full campaign} The sequential DE, versions 1 and 2 and jDE were run on the
36 configurations (4 functions, $D\in\{10,50,100\}$, $N\in\{50,100,500\}$, 10 seeds), 1440
runs in total (CUDA 13, sequential code compiled with \texttt{g++ -O2}); all passed the
built-in checks. The complete tables (mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation per
configuration) are in the repository (\texttt{results/analyse}).

\emph{Time.} Table~\ref{tab:camp} and Fig.~\ref{fig:camp}. Version~2 is the fastest
program in all 36 configurations; its speedup over the sequential code grows with $D$
and $N$, from $1.5\times$ (Rosenbrock, $D=10$, $N=50$) to $74.5\times$ (Rastrigin, $D=100$,
$N=500$). Version~1 only pays off with $N=500$ ($6.0\times$ to $9.5\times$); with $N=50$ it
ranges from $0.7\times$ to $1.5\times$. jDE costs 10 to 33\,\% more time than version~2, depending on the configuration (13 to
32\,\% on the pooled times of Table~\ref{tab:camp}). These speedups are at equal budget of
evaluations, not at equal quality: where versions 1 and 2 are less accurate (see below), the
sequential DE gets more from the same budget.

\emph{Quality.} Table~\ref{tab:err} gives the mean and standard deviation of the final
error for $N=100$.
Each algorithm was compared with the sequential DE (Mann--Whitney, Holm
correction within each configuration, $\alpha=0.05$). Version~1: 0 better, 29 not
different, 7 worse; version~2: 1, 27, 8; jDE: 19, 15, 2 (with Holm's correction over all
108 tests: 0/31/5, 0/32/4 and 17/18/1). Among the three implementations of DE/rand/1/bin,
Kruskal--Wallis detects a difference in 9 configurations out of 36, six of them with
$N=500$ (Sphere for the three dimensions, Griewank for $D=50$ and $100$, Rosenbrock for
$D=50$), where the sequential DE
reaches an error 1.1 to 4 times lower (e.g.\ $1.1\times10^{-4}$ against $2.3\times10^{-4}$ for
Sphere, $D=50$). With $N=500$ the budget allows five times fewer generations than with
$N=100$ (200 to 2000) and the runs have not converged; our explanation was that the
asynchronous replacement of the sequential code propagates improvements within a
generation, which the generational GPU versions cannot do. To test it, we ran the sequential
program in a generational mode (\texttt{--generationnel}: trials read the previous
population and winners are written to a second one, as on the GPU) on the 36
configurations with the same 10 seeds, 360 runs. The generational sequential DE differs from
the asynchronous one (Mann--Whitney, raw $p<0.05$) in exactly the 9 configurations where
Kruskal--Wallis separates the three implementations, and it is not distinguishable from
version~1 or from version~2 in 35 of the 36 configurations. The loss of accuracy therefore
comes from the replacement scheme, not from the GPU implementation. With $N\le100$, no difference is detected in 21 of 24 configurations.
jDE solves Rastrigin for $D=50$ and $D=100$ with $N=100$ (error 0), where DE/rand/1/bin
stays near 165 and 535. Counting as a success a final error below $10^{-8}$, as Qin
\emph{et al.}, the sequential DE and versions 1 and 2 succeed in 39\,\% of the 360 runs
each and jDE in 62\,\%. This gain is expected on our benchmark: the functions are unrotated and
two of them are separable, which favours a small $CR$ (Table~\ref{tab:var}: $CR=0.1$
reaches $10^{-11}$ on Rastrigin), and jDE adapts $CR$ towards such values; on the
non-separable Rosenbrock with $D=100$, $N=100$, jDE is worse than the sequential DE in mean
($112$ against $89.6$). The gain of jDE is algorithmic, not a property of the GPU.

\emph{Comparison with cudaDEi.} Qin \emph{et al.}~\cite{qin2012} use the same protocol
(DE/rand/1/bin, $F=0.5$, $CR=0.3$, $10^4D$ evaluations, same functions and sizes, 25 runs,
GTX~560). Their reported times are 59 to 365 times ours; with GPUs seven years apart and
unreported details (floating-point precision, timed region), this ratio does not measure
the quality of either implementation. For $N\le100$ the errors are equal on Sphere, equal or lower for
us on Griewank (0 against 0.001 to 0.005), and equal on Rastrigin for $D=10$, but cudaDEi reports much lower
errors on Rastrigin for $D\ge50$ (2.94 against 165 for $D=50$, $N=100$), on Rosenbrock in
most configurations, and with $N=500$.
An independent NumPy implementation of DE/rand/1/bin gives 147 to 174 on Rastrigin
($D=50$, $N=100$) whatever the bound handling, and 80 with ten times the budget, so our
values are those expected from this algorithm; we could not identify the cause of the
gap, which the paper's description does not allow us to reproduce.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conc}
Mapping one individual to one thread is enough to obtain a correct GPU DE, but not a
fast one: with populations of 50 to 100 individuals, the GPU is almost idle. Mapping
one individual to one block and one gene to one thread, fusing the four DE
operators in a single kernel and keeping the population and the best-individual
search on the device gives a further $1.7\times$ to $14.6\times$ on a Tesla T4, and
$35\times$ over a sequential implementation (Rastrigin, $D=100$, $N=100$), up to $74.5\times$
with $N=500$. No significant difference in quality is detected in 21 of the 24
configurations with $N\le100$.
Measured timings, not theoretical occupancy, determined the block size.
Limitations: the speedups come from a single GPU and single-core sequential code
compiled with \texttt{-O2}; quality was compared on 10 runs per configuration; the block-size rule was fitted on 12 cases; the
sequential and GPU programs are two variants (asynchronous and generational) of
DE/rand/1/bin, and the speedups are at equal budget, not at equal quality; no profiler
was used, so the explanations based on occupancy and memory accesses are consistent
with the timings but not measured directly; the benchmark contains no rotated or
composite function. Among the variants, the self-adaptive jDE gave the best overall
ranking at $D=50$, $N=100$; this single configuration does not establish a general
ranking. Over the full campaign, version~2 is the fastest program in every
configuration and jDE is the only variant that improves significantly on the sequential
DE; the generational GPU versions lose some accuracy against the asynchronous
sequential DE when the population is large and the number of generations small, and a
generational sequential DE loses exactly the same accuracy, which locates the cause in
the replacement scheme.

Future work: overlapping kernels P and MCER on two streams as in cudaDEi; reading the
shift vector through the read-only cache instead of constant memory; warp-level reductions
(\texttt{\_\_shfl\_down\_sync}) and several individuals per block when $D$ is small; profiling
with Nsight Compute; equivalence tests and effect sizes instead of non-significance alone;
rotated and composite CEC functions and modern adaptive DE (SHADE, L-SHADE).

The code, the raw results of the 1440 runs and the generational control, and the analysis
scripts are available at \url{https://github.com/amenacer/DE_CUDA}.
